Explainer — the shape factor k

← Elective (RTT) spine · How the model works · Explainers hub

Two waiting lists can be the same size, fed by the same number of referrals, and still perform completely differently on the 18-week standard — because what matters is not just how many people are waiting but how long they have each been waiting: the list's age mix. The shape factor k is the one number the model uses to capture the age mix. Your instinct that it is "a distribution modifier" is exactly right: k measures how far a real list's age distribution departs from the textbook shape, and the model then stretches its size-vs-performance arithmetic by that factor.

1  The puzzle — same size, different health

Imagine two specialties, each with 2,000 patients waiting and 100 referrals arriving a week. In the first, bookings roughly follow arrival order: nobody is ancient, and 70% of the list is under 18 weeks. In the second, a few hundred long-waiters have quietly accumulated at the back: only 45% is under 18 weeks. Size alone cannot tell these lists apart. A model that worked from size and referrals only would treat them identically — and be wrong about both. k is how this model tells them apart using only published data.

2  The textbook shape — and k as the "how far from textbook" dial

Queueing theory has a natural default: if the next patient is picked effectively at random (a realistic stand-in for clinically-led mixed booking), the ages of people on the list follow the exponential curve — lots of recent joiners, a long thin tail. On that textbook shape, the size of the list and the referral rate fully determine the %<18wk. So the model asks each specialty one question: "given your size and referrals, is your published %<18wk what the textbook predicts?" The answer is k:

  • k = 1 — the list behaves exactly as its size suggests (textbook shape).
  • k > 1 — the list is younger than its size suggests: disciplined, FIFO-like booking keeps everyone moving (or the list was recently validated). It performs like a textbook list only 1/k as large.
  • k < 1 — the list has a fatter long-wait tail than its size suggests: long-waiters are accumulating at the back.

Formally, for the 18-week window: k = −ln(1 − p) × L ÷ (18 × λ), where p is the published %<18wk, L the list size and λ the effective referral rate. A worked example with real numbers — the trust's largest list, straight from the seed:

5  What the model does with k — and what to watch

  • Calibration: k is measured once per specialty at the model start and anchors each trajectory to the published %<18wk — so the model never claims a specialty is healthier or sicker than its published position.
  • Held constant by default: booking discipline is assumed to stay as it is to 2030. That is a stated assumption, not a fact — the booking-discipline drift lever on the RTT page morphs k linearly by 2029 to test it, and the sensitivity tornado shows what ±20% of drift does to the headline (because the sustainable list scales linearly with k, drift towards FIFO shrinks the clearance ask and drift towards tail-neglect grows it).
  • k conflates discipline with data quality. A very high k can mean genuinely disciplined booking — or a recently validated list. NUH's highest-k specialty sits just after the Feb–Mar 2026 EPR migration purge removed thousands of stale pathways, so treat extreme values with respect for both readings.
  • One number, one distribution. k summarises a whole age distribution in a single ratio at the 18-week point. Two lists can share a k and differ elsewhere in the tail; per-patient PTL data would replace this approximation entirely.
